Our Discoveries

Research Findings

The forest speaks in a thousand voices if we but listen. For three years now, I have sat among the trees of Gombe, watching our closest relatives move through their world with a grace and complexity that challenges what we thought we knew about being alive on this planet. What I have witnessed is not merely animal behavior—it is the unfolding story of consciousness itself, a narrative we share more deeply than we once imagined.

Tool Making & Usage

When I first observed David Greybeard fashioning and using tools to extract termites, the boundary between human and animal began to dissolve before my eyes. These weren't random acts but deliberate crafting—stems carefully selected, stripped of leaves, and fashioned to just the right length. Each tool bears the signature of its maker and purpose, a humble technology born of necessity and intelligence. What we call "human" is perhaps better understood as a pattern that flows through all life, expressing itself in varying degrees of complexity.

Social Structures & Relationships

The chimpanzee community reveals itself as a living tapestry of relationships—mothers and children, allies and rivals, all woven together in patterns that stretch across generations. I've documented complex coalitions forming and dissolving, grudges held for years, and reconciliations marked by tender embraces. In their social fluidity, I see reflections of our own societies, suggesting that the roots of our politics and ethics may reach deeper than our civilizations. The boundaries between their social awareness and ours blur with each day of observation.

Key Observations That Challenge Convention

01

Meat Consumption & Hunting

The prevailing wisdom that chimpanzees were strictly vegetarian has fallen away as I've documented coordinated hunting parties pursuing colobus monkeys through the canopy. This is not mindless predation but strategic cooperation—with sharing behaviors that follow successful hunts, creating bonds between individuals. The line between herbivore and carnivore reveals itself as another of our artificial categories that nature refuses to honor.

02

Individual Personalities

Each chimpanzee emerges in my notes as unmistakably themselves—Flo with her fierce maternal protectiveness, Figan with his political intelligence, David Greybeard with his gentle curiosity. These are not interchangeable units of a species but unique beings navigating their world through the lens of their distinct temperaments. In acknowledging their individuality, we must also acknowledge the ethical implications of how we regard these fellow travelers on Earth's journey.

03

Communication Systems

Their world pulses with meaning conveyed through gesture, vocalization, and touch. I have documented over 20 distinct calls, each contextual, each carrying information vital to community cohesion. This is not the absence of language but perhaps its embryonic form—or more accurately, a different mode of communion that humans, in our fixation on words, have largely forgotten how to hear. The forest itself becomes a medium through which meaning flows between beings.

The Methodological Revolution

Traditional scientific approaches demanded distance, detachment, and even the assignment of numbers rather than names. But to truly understand these beings, I have chosen a different path—one of patient relationship-building and recognition of each individual's unique presence in the world. In this approach, observation becomes not an act of extraction but of communion.

"When I look into the eyes of a chimpanzee, I see an intelligent being looking back. They deserve a special status, a special consideration that recognizes the shadow of ourselves we see in them."

Behavioral Flexibility

Adaptation to changing conditions through learned behaviors rather than genetic changes, demonstrating cognitive plasticity.

Cultural Transmission

Knowledge passed between generations through observation and teaching, forming distinct cultural traditions within communities.

Emotional Intelligence

Complex emotional lives including grief, joy, jealousy, and reconciliation behaviors that maintain community bonds.

The Community

Meet the Chimpanzees

Each morning as the mist rises from the forest floor, I am greeted not by specimens but by persons—each with their own story, their own way of moving through the world. These are not merely objects of study but sovereign beings with whom I share this time and place. In their eyes I recognize consciousness looking back at me, asking questions of their own. Here you will meet some of the remarkable individuals who have allowed me into their lives, each one teaching me something about what it means to be alive on this shared Earth.

David Greybeard

"The Bridge-Builder"

The first to trust me enough to approach, David Greybeard became the doorway through which I entered the chimpanzee world. It was his gentle acceptance that encouraged others to overcome their fear. When I think of wisdom embodied, I see his thoughtful face with its distinctive silver chin, his measured movements, his patient observation before action.

David was the first chimpanzee I observed using tools—carefully selecting stems, modifying them, and using them to extract termites from their mounds. This moment dissolved the supposed boundary between human and animal technology, challenging centuries of human exceptionalism in a single, elegant motion of his hand.

Age: ~15 years Status: Respected Male

Flo

"The Matriarch"

If the community has a center, it might be found in Flo—a mother whose influence flows through multiple generations. With her distinctive bulbous nose and torn ears that speak of a long life's journey, she carries herself with the quiet authority of one who has seen much of what the forest has to offer, both its abundance and its hardships.

Her maternal style reveals something profound about chimpanzee society—affectionate, protective, yet encouraging independence in her children. Through Flo, I witness how knowledge passes between generations, not through books or formal instruction, but through the intimate dance of daily life shared between mother and child. Her relationship with her offspring Fifi, Figan, and infant Flint offers a mirror in which we might better understand our own evolutionary history.

Age: ~28 years Status: Matriarch

Goliath

"The Challenger"

In Goliath's powerful presence, I witness the complex interplay of strength and restraint that governs chimpanzee social structures. His displays—charging through the forest, dragging branches, drumming on buttress roots—speak to his ambition and vitality. Yet even in his most impressive displays, there remains a choreography, a sense of communication rather than mere intimidation.

Currently second to Mike in the dominance hierarchy, Goliath's relationship with other males reveals the ever-shifting alliances and tensions that weave through the community. Power here is not simply about physical strength but about building coalitions, about knowing when to display and when to reconcile. In his political maneuvering, I see reflections of our own social structures, suggesting that the roots of politics reach deeper than civilization.

Age: ~16 years Status: Beta Male

Mike

"The Innovator"

How does an individual transform their position within a complex social web? Mike's remarkable rise to alpha status offers a case study in innovation and strategic thinking. Once a low-ranking male, Mike discovered that empty kerosene cans from our camp made his displays exponentially more intimidating—a technological adaptation that fundamentally shifted his social standing.

In Mike's strategic appropriation of human objects, I observe something profound about adaptability and the cognitive flexibility to recognize opportunity in the unfamiliar. His story challenges simple narratives about dominance, suggesting that intelligence and adaptability can sometimes overcome raw physical power. The social structure bends around innovation, just as human societies have been shaped by technological leaps throughout our history.

Age: ~14 years Status: Alpha Male

Figan

"The Strategist"

Son of Flo, brother of Fifi and Flint, Figan moves through the community with a keen social intelligence that makes him fascinating to observe. While not physically imposing, he navigates the complex web of relationships with a political acumen that suggests depth of understanding beyond mere instinct or conditioning.

I have watched him form alliances, wait for opportune moments, and use his observations of others to strategic advantage. His relationship with his brother Faben—who has a partially paralyzed arm—reveals a capacity for cooperation that extends beyond immediate self-interest. Together, they coordinate in ways that complement each other's strengths and weaknesses, suggesting an understanding of differing abilities and mutual benefit.

Age: ~9 years Status: Adolescent Male

Fifi

"The Observer"

Flo's adolescent daughter Fifi moves through the forest with a quiet attentiveness that seems to absorb everything around her. In her watchful presence, I recognize the vital importance of social learning—how she studies her mother's techniques for termite fishing, for selecting medicinal plants, for navigating complex social interactions.

As she transitions toward adulthood, she carries her mother's knowledge forward, not as a passive inheritance but as a living tradition modified by her own experiences and insights. Through Fifi, I witness how knowledge accumulates across generations, creating cultural continuity that exists beyond any individual life span. Her story is a reminder that we humans are not the only species to build upon the wisdom of ancestors.

Age: ~7 years Status: Adolescent Female

March 17, 1966

Peak of Dry Season

0530 - 1830 hrs
Ridge Trail to Valley Stream
Flo's Group

Entry #247

Dawn broke over the valley with that particular amber light that seems to illuminate the world from within rather than without. I found Flo and her family still nested in the great fig tree that overhangs the eastern ravine. The soft sounds of their awakening—the rustle of nest leaves, the gentle hoots that pass between mother and child—serve as counterpoint to the dawn chorus of birds.

As they descended, I watched Flint (now 3.5 years) imitate his mother's careful testing of branches. There is nothing automatic in this learning—it is a deliberate transmission, a curriculum written in gestures rather than words. Fifi watched from above, then took a more daring route down, her adolescent confidence both mirroring and diverging from her mother's caution. In these subtly different approaches to the same necessity, I glimpse how innovation enters tradition, how knowledge evolves across generations.

The morning centered around a termite mound near the stream crossing. For two hours, I watched three generations engaged in the same activity, each with their distinctive style. Flo's movements were economical, practiced—each probe carefully selected and modified with minimal effort. Fifi worked with greater concentration, her focus almost palpable as she refined her technique through trial and adjustment. Young Flint alternated between serious imitation and playful experimentation, sometimes using the tools properly, sometimes inventing games with them.

Most revealing was a moment when Flint's tool broke. Rather than immediately fashioning another, he turned to Flo with a soft whimper. She paused in her own fishing, watched him for a moment, then deliberately slowed her movements as she created a new tool—as if offering a clearer demonstration for his benefit. Is this teaching? The boundary between incidental learning and intentional instruction blurs in such moments, challenging our assumptions about the uniqueness of human pedagogy.

April 2, 1966

Early Rains

0615 - 1745 hrs
Northern Grove
Mike, David Greybeard, Goliath

Entry #261

The first substantial rain of the season transformed the forest today. The percussion of water on leaves created a sound chamber in which all other activities seemed to take place. Even the calls of the chimpanzees took on different acoustic properties, their hoots and pants resonating differently in the moisture-laden air.

What I witnessed during the downpour challenges conventional wisdom about chimpanzee responses to rain. Rather than seeking immediate shelter, Mike, David Greybeard, and Goliath remained in the open for nearly ten minutes as the storm intensified. Their behavior shifted subtly—movement patterns becoming more fluid, vocalizations dropping to softer registers. David Greybeard extended his hand palm-up into the rain repeatedly, bringing drops to his mouth. Was this simply opportunistic drinking, or something more—perhaps a form of sensory engagement with the changing conditions?

Most remarkable was what followed the heaviest rainfall. As the clouds broke and sunlight streamed through in defined shafts, Goliath initiated what I can only describe as play. He began picking up the newly fallen palm fruits, made slippery by the rain, and sliding them across a smooth, exposed root system. When the fruits gathered in a natural depression, he would retrieve them and begin again. Soon David Greybeard joined, and for twenty minutes they engaged in this seemingly purposeless but clearly absorbing activity.

Even Mike, usually so focused on maintaining his status through displays of strength, paused to watch and eventually participated—his typical dominance behaviors temporarily suspended in this shared engagement with their transformed environment. In such moments, the boundary between necessary activity and what we might call aesthetic appreciation becomes wonderfully blurred. These beings do not merely survive in their world—they experience it, respond to its changes, and sometimes appear to savor its sensory offerings beyond any immediate survival value.

April 17, 1966

Rainy Season

0550 - 1815 hrs
Western Ridge
Mixed Group - Hunt

Entry #272

Today I witnessed the forest's ancient choreography of life and death—a coordinated hunt that challenges any simplistic understanding of chimpanzee social behavior. The morning began with distant pant-hoots echoing from the western ridge, a chorus of voices converging from different directions. By mid-morning, fourteen individuals had gathered in a loose association that seemed to pulse with collective anticipation.

What followed was not chaos but coordination. Without visible signals I could discern, individuals began taking positions that effectively surrounded a section of forest known to harbor colobus monkey troops. Mike and three adult males formed the center of the approach, while Figan and two others moved to positions that would cut off potential escape routes. Even females normally uninvolved in hunting took peripheral positions, their presence narrowing the colobus's options.

The question that haunts me: How is this synchrony achieved without language as we understand it? There were no vocalized commands, no single individual directing others. Instead, a distributed awareness seemed to flow through the group—each participant both acting and responding, leading and following in turns. This suggests a mode of social cognition that transcends our traditional categories of instinct versus planning.

Most revealing was the aftermath. The successful hunt yielded a single juvenile colobus, not enough to provide significant nutrition to all present. Yet the distribution followed patterns that reflected social bonds rather than simply dominance. David Greybeard received choice portions despite not being the highest-ranking male, his alliances with both Mike and several mature females seemingly influencing the outcome. A young female with a new infant was given access before some higher-ranking males—a deviation from the expected pattern that suggests nuanced social calculations beyond mere hierarchy.

In the sharing of meat, I glimpse something that might be called ethics—not in the human philosophical sense, but in the embodied wisdom of a social structure that has evolved to balance individual need against group cohesion. The seeds of our own moral systems perhaps lie in such moments, not as their cause but as their distant echo.

Observation Methodology

My approach defies conventional scientific distance. Rather than observing from predetermined intervals with clipboard in hand, I move with the rhythms of chimpanzee life—waiting when they wait, traveling when they travel, attending to what captures their attention rather than imposing my human categories of significance.

This method requires dismantling the wall between observer and observed—becoming, in a sense, transparent to their experience while remaining conscious of one's own presence as an element within their social field. The aim is not to disappear but to participate respectfully in a multispecies community where humans are not the measure of all things.

Habituation Without Interference

Building trust through consistent, non-threatening presence while minimizing disruption to natural behaviors.

Individual Recognition

Naming and tracking individuals to understand social relationships and personality differences.

Extended Immersion

Long-term presence allows observation of rare events and recognition of patterns invisible in short-term studies.

Contextual Documentation

Recording environmental factors, social context, and emotional states alongside behavioral observations.

Photography Approach

Each photograph emerges from hours of patient waiting, from the slow dissolution of novelty into familiarity. The camera becomes not an instrument of capture but of witness—a technological extension of attention that allows moments of connection to be shared beyond the boundaries of time and place.

This is not documentation in the traditional scientific sense of creating specimens for analysis. Rather, these images serve as invitations to relationship—windows through which others might glimpse not just the physical forms of chimpanzees but their presence as beings with whom we share both differences and profound kinship.

Non-intrusive Presence

Photographs taken only after sufficient habituation, using minimal equipment to reduce disturbance.

Ethical Documentation

Prioritizing chimpanzee wellbeing over photographic opportunity, never baiting or manipulating behavior.

Context Preservation

Capturing not only individuals but their relationships to one another and to their environment.

Narrative Integrity

Using images to tell honest stories rather than reinforce preconceptions about animal behavior.

Primary Camera

Leica M3 with 50mm lens — chosen for its silent operation and mechanical reliability in humid conditions. The manual focus encourages deliberate composition and patience.

Field Techniques

Natural light only, avoiding flash to prevent disturbance. Long lenses (135mm) allow distance while capturing detail. Slower film stock (Kodak Tri-X 400) provides more natural grain structure.

Documentation System

Each photograph paired with detailed field notes recording date, time, individuals present, behaviors observed, and environmental conditions. Creating context beyond the visual frame.
